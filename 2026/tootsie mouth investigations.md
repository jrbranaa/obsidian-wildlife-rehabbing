# Tootsie Mouth Investigations
**Patient:** Tootsie — Western Grey Squirrel (*Sciurus griseus*), ~8 weeks old
**Concern:** Possible obstruction in mouth or cheek pouch

---

## Quick Reference: When to Call a Vet Immediately

- Labored or open-mouth breathing (squirrels are obligate nasal breathers — any mouth breathing is an emergency)
- Blue or pale gums
- Loss of consciousness or extreme lethargy
- Suspected throat/esophageal obstruction with complete inability to swallow
- Signs of infection (swelling, pus, fever, severe pain response)

---

## Materials You Will Need

### Examination Kit

**Lighting & Visualization**
- Small penlight or headlamp (hands-free preferred)
- Otoscope with a small plastic cone tip — the best tool for illuminating the narrow rodent oral cavity; a Welch Allyn illuminated nasal speculum works even better if available
- Magnifying glass or loupe (optional but helpful for detail)

**Restraint**
- Small soft fleece square or hand towel for "burrito" wrap (approximately 12" × 12")
- A helper (second pair of hands strongly recommended)

**Improvised Jaw Gag (WildCare Method)**
- 1 standard paper clip
- Soft vetwrap (cohesive bandage) — cut a small strip
- Wrap the paper clip fully in vetwrap so no metal is exposed. The flat profile slides in easily; rotate to vertical inside the mouth to gently prop the jaws apart.
- *Alternatives:* two blunt-ended toothpicks padded with gauze, or the padded handle end of a cotton swab — whatever creates the gentlest, smallest gap

**Inspection Aids**
- Cotton-tipped applicators (Q-tips) — for clearing saliva and gently moving tissue aside
- Small dental mirror (optional) — for viewing deeper into the oral cavity

---

### Obstruction Removal Kit

**Flushing Supplies**
- 0.9% saline (sterile) — for flushing; warm to body temperature (~37°C / 99°F) before use
- 1 mL and 3 mL syringes (no needle) — for directing a gentle saline stream into the cheek or mouth
- Small bowl or cup to warm the saline (set syringe in warm water for a few minutes)
- Paper towels or gauze squares — to catch expelled material and absorb drainage

**Manual Extraction Tools**
- Fine-tipped **blunt** hemostatic forceps (curved or straight) — blunt tips are essential; sharp-tipped forceps can injure delicate cheek pouch mucosa
- Cotton-tipped applicators — for repositioning loosened material and applying topical antiseptic
- Small gauze pads (2×2") — for wiping the mouth area and catching debris

**Topical Antiseptic (Post-Removal)**
- 0.12% chlorhexidine gluconate oral rinse (dilute from standard 2% solution if needed) — apply with a cotton swab to reduce bacterial load after clearing material
- *Do not use full-strength chlorhexidine — dilute appropriately*

**Warming Aid**
- Warm damp cloth or small warm pack — apply to the exterior of the cheek for 30–60 seconds before flushing to soften hardened food material

- Small notebook or chart — to document findings, date, and any material removed

## Signs & Symptoms of Something Stuck in the Mouth or Throat

Watch for any combination of the following:

**Behavioral signs:**
- Repeatedly pawing or rubbing at the face/mouth
- Head tossing, head tilting, or shaking the head
- Odd chewing motions in the air without food present
- Gagging, retching, or repeated swallowing attempts
- Sudden refusal to eat or drink after previously feeding normally
- Distress vocalizations
- Agitation or restlessness disproportionate to handling

**Physical/visible signs:**
- Visibly asymmetrical cheek — one side bulging more than the other, especially if the bulge is not emptying over time
- Drooling or wet fur around the chin, chest, or forepaws (indicates the squirrel cannot swallow normally)
- Discharge or foul odor from the mouth
- Audible clicking, gurgling, or labored sounds when breathing or swallowing
- Reluctance to open mouth or strong resistance to facial touch (pain response)
- Facial swelling, especially around the jaw or cheek
- Weight loss or failure to thrive
- Regurgitation of formula/food shortly after eating

**Note for 8-week-olds:** At this age Tootsie is transitioning from formula to solid food. Choking hazards at this stage include: seed skins, fruit strings, nut shell fragments, primate biscuit pieces, and bedding material. Even soft food can pack into a developing cheek pouch and cause impaction.

## Western Grey Squirrel: Relevant Anatomy at 8 Weeks

**Dentition at ~8 weeks:**
- Lower incisors erupt around 3 weeks; upper incisors between 4–6 weeks; cheek teeth (premolars/molars) appear around 6 weeks. By 8 weeks all primary teeth should be present.
- Dental formula: I 1/1, C 0/0, PM 2/1, M 3/3 × 2 = 22 teeth
- Incisors are continuously growing (hypsodont) — they must align and wear properly or malocclusion develops rapidly
- Teeth at this age are still relatively small and delicate

**Cheek pouches:**
- Western grey squirrels do have cheek pouches, though less dramatically developed than ground squirrels. They can store food that may pack and harden (impaction), especially during the transition to solid food.
- The oral opening is proportionally narrow compared to carnivores; cheek teeth are positioned far caudally, making the back of the mouth difficult to visualize.

**Important:** Squirrels are obligate nasal breathers. Any oral or pharyngeal obstruction that affects airflow through the nares is a respiratory emergency.

## Examination Technique

### Preparation
- Warm, quiet environment — minimize stress, which worsens respiratory effort
- Have a small penlight or headlamp ready
- Have a helper if possible — one person restrains, one examines
- Prepare warm saline (0.9% NaCl) in a small syringe for flushing if needed
- Have fine-tipped blunt forceps nearby (not sharply pointed)

### Restraint
Wrap Tootsie firmly but gently in a soft towel or fleece "burrito" — body and legs contained, head exposed. This prevents thrashing, which could injure both the squirrel and the examiner. Keep the head level or slightly elevated; avoid tilting the nose downward if an obstruction is suspected.

### Mouth Opening Technique (Rehabber Method)
Wildlife care staff have described a useful improvised tool for holding a rodent's mouth open for examination:

> Construct a small gag from a paper clip covered in soft vet wrap (vetwrap). The flat profile allows it to slide in easily, then rotate it vertically to gently open the jaws. **Do not force.** Use the minimum gap needed to see inside.

Alternatively, two rounded toothpick ends, a small padded tongue depressor, or the blunt end of a cotton swab can be used to prop the jaws apart very gently.

**Do not attempt mouth opening if:**
- The squirrel is in severe distress or near collapse
- You suspect a jaw fracture (do not add lateral force)
- The animal is showing signs of respiratory crisis — go to emergency vet immediately

### Visual Inspection
Once the mouth is open:
1. Use a penlight or otoscope to illuminate the oral cavity
2. Check the incisors — are they aligned, intact, and similar in length? Shortened, blunted, or backward-curving upper incisors can suggest odontoma (bony overgrowth that can obstruct the nasal passage and cause secondary respiratory distress)
3. Gently inspect the cheek pouches on both sides — press lightly from the outside of the cheek inward to evert the pouch contents
4. Look for: food impaction, foreign material (bedding, bark, shell fragments), wounds, swelling, redness, abscess, or unusual growths
5. Check the back of the throat as far as safely visible — look for lodged material, swelling, or discoloration
6. Note any smell — foul odor indicates fermentation, infection, or necrotic tissue

### Palpation (External)
- Gently palpate both cheeks simultaneously for asymmetry, firmness, or pain response
- Palpate along the ventral mandible (lower jaw border) for swelling or bony irregularity
- Check the temporomandibular joint areas for swelling
- Assess jaw mobility gently — can Tootsie open and close without grinding or resistance?

## Approaches to Removing Obstructions

### Step 1: Gentle External Expression (Try First)
For a cheek pouch that appears packed with food:
- Warm a small damp cloth and apply to the cheek for 30–60 seconds to soften the contents
- Apply gentle external pressure from the back of the cheek forward (like squeezing a tube of toothpaste), working material toward the mouth opening
- Have a cotton ball or gauze ready to catch expelled material
- **Do not apply strong force** — cheek pouch mucosa tears easily

### Step 2: Warm Saline Flush
- Using a 1–3 mL syringe (no needle) with 0.9% saline warmed to body temperature (~37°C / 99°F)
- Direct a gentle stream into the cheek area or back of mouth while the squirrel's head is slightly tilted to allow drainage
- This can loosen hardened or packed food material
- Allow drainage — do not let fluid pool, which risks aspiration
- Repeat gently as needed

### Step 3: Manual Removal with Forceps
- Use fine-tipped **blunt** forceps (not sharp points)
- If visible material is present in the pouch or mouth, gently grasp and extract
- When squirrels feel material being removed, they often reflexively chew and relax the mouth, which can aid extraction
- Work slowly; do not pull forcefully if material is adhered
- After removal, gently flush with saline to clear residue

### Step 4: Veterinary Intervention (Sedation/Anesthesia)
If the above steps fail, or if you cannot safely visualize the obstruction, or if there is any sign of infection or deep tissue involvement, sedation is required for safe examination and treatment.

**What a vet can do:**
- Use isoflurane gas anesthesia (2.5–5% induction) — relatively safe for small mammals
- Place a rodent mouth gag/speculum for full oral exam
- Use an otoscope or illuminated nasal speculum for visualization
- Use an endoscope with fine forceps for deeper or esophageal foreign bodies
- Flush the pouch with antimicrobial solution if infection is present
- Prescribe systemic antibiotics if infection is confirmed
- Take dental radiographs if malocclusion or odontoma is suspected

**Anesthesia note:** Ketamine + xylazine (IM or SQ) is documented for ground squirrels and related species for short procedures (~20–30 minutes). Isoflurane is preferred for controllable depth. Always have a wildlife-experienced vet perform or supervise sedation.

## After Obstruction Removal: Supportive Care

### Monitor for Infection
If material was impacted, there is a risk of fermentation and bacterial overgrowth. Watch for:
- Continued swelling or new swelling after treatment
- Foul-smelling breath
- Lethargy or loss of appetite not improving within 12–24 hours
- Any pus or discharge from the mouth

If infection is suspected, consult a vet about systemic antibiotics appropriate for squirrels (e.g., Trimethoprim-sulfa, Amoxicillin-clavulanate — dosing must come from your rehabber vet).

### Oral Antiseptic
A dilute chlorhexidine oral rinse (0.12% solution) can be applied gently to the oral cavity on a cotton swab to reduce bacterial load after clearing an impaction. Avoid letting the squirrel ingest large amounts.

### Feeding Adjustments After Removal
- Return to formula-only feeding for 12–24 hours to rest the oral tissues
- When reintroducing solids, offer only very soft, moist foods initially (mashed avocado-free vegetables, soft primate biscuit pieces moistened with formula, etc.)
- Remove choking hazards: seed skins, fruit strings, shell fragments, fibrous vegetable strings
- Ensure fresh water is always available (8-week-olds transitioning to solid food need hydration access)
- **Never cut formula off prematurely** — always let the squirrel lead weaning

### Dental/Tooth Monitoring
Because Tootsie's teeth are rapidly developing at 8 weeks, monitor weekly:
- Are upper and lower incisors meeting properly?
- Are teeth wearing evenly?
- Any tooth appearing stunted, blunted, curved inward, or growing sideways warrants veterinary dental evaluation
- Malocclusion at this age can develop quickly and cause chronic oral obstruction, inability to eat, and weight loss

### Signs of Odontoma to Watch For
Odontoma is a bony growth around the tooth root that can block the nasal sinus and cause respiratory distress. Early signs include:
- Runny nose or "cold-like" symptoms
- Upper incisors that appear short, blunted, or curving backward
- Breathing sounds, especially nasal congestion
- Gradual inability to eat

Odontoma requires veterinary diagnosis (dental X-ray) and is not treatable at home.

## When to Seek Veterinary Help Immediately

Do not delay in contacting a wildlife vet or your supervising rehabber vet if:
- You cannot identify or reach the obstruction safely
- Symptoms are not improving within a few hours of home treatment
- Tootsie cannot swallow or eat at all
- Any sign of respiratory distress (open-mouth breathing, wheezing, blue gums)
- Swelling is increasing
- There is blood, pus, or obvious tissue damage in the mouth
- Tootsie becomes lethargic, cold, or unresponsive
