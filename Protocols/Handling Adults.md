Adult squirrels move fast and can bite regardless of how badly injured they
are — handling is fundamentally different from a hand-raised juvenile.

## Initial approach

- Let a just-received adult **settle 1-2 hours** before examining, unless
  bleeding severely or struggling to breathe (immediate treatment needed).
- Aconite + Arnica (homeopathic) can be given in the meantime.
- Keep warm (heating pad Low), quiet, dark — towel/blanket over the
  carrier/cage.
- Assess willingness to eat/drink and movement in the carrier before forcing
  a hands-on exam.

## Handling technique

- **Heavy, Kevlar-lined gloves** and/or wrap firmly in a small heavy blanket
  — always. Protective clothing (heavy shirt/sweatshirt); keep fingers out of
  glove tips.
- "Bundle" front-to-back in fleece/blanket, hold firmly: one gloved hand over
  the shoulder area to control the head, the other at the hindquarters.
- Peel the fleece back section by section to examine limbs/body/head while
  keeping him restrained.
- Get help from another rehabber when possible — recommended even for
  experienced handlers.

## Reading the animal

- Warning signs before an attack: tail-flashing, chattering, foot-stamping.
- Alternative defense: holding still, not blinking ("playing dead") — factor
  this into how you read a physical exam.
- If he becomes hysterical: pause a few minutes or return him to the basket
  and try again later, rather than pushing through.

## Exam environment

- Wide, flat surface, closed room, good light.
- **Never leave any squirrel unattended**, even briefly, unless securely
  latched in a cage/carrier.

## Bite risk

Squirrel bite force is roughly **1,000 psi** — can bite through anything but
Kevlar. A bite is extremely painful and typically requires a **tetanus shot**
and likely antibiotics for the handler — keep tetanus vaccination current.
See [[Rehabber Safety]] for the broader zoonotic/bite-risk picture.
